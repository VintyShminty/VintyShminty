## Research: copyright and originality (separate session, branch `main`, no code)

### What was done

- Researched what copyright and related law means for the app's look and feel: how different from Vinted it has to be, and what else must be our own. Summary below so Eden can come back to it and criticise his own thinking.

#### Research summary: copying Vinted, what is allowed

The short version: nobody owns how an app works. People own how it looks, what it is called and what it says. Same skeleton, different skin and different voice.

**Free to copy (ideas and common patterns)**
- Features: listing items, favourites, offers, chat, bundles, buyer protection, reviews, following sellers.
- Flows: the order of steps, for example photos, then title, then category, then price.
- Standard layouts: a tab bar along the bottom, a grid of item cards, filter chips, a heart to save, a search bar on top.
- Category trees and filters (women, men, kids, size, brand, condition).

**Must be our own (the expression)**
- Name, logo and app icon. These are trademarks and the biggest risk. The final name needs a trademark search before launch.
- Brand colour as identity. Vinted's teal is recognisable. Our espresso accent is already clearly different.
- Illustrations, onboarding pictures, empty screen drawings, badges, mascots.
- Custom icons. Generic free sets like Lucide are fine. Never redraw Vinted's own icons.
- Every word: buttons, help text, emails, terms of service, policies. Write our own, even when the meaning is the same.
- Photos, sounds and animations from their app or marketing.
- Their code and their data. Never scrape their listings or users.

**Grey zone: the whole package**
- Each piece can be fine alone while the whole still looks like a clone. The test: cover the logo, would a normal person think it is Vinted? If yes, change things.
- EU design rights: Vinted is an EU company. Screen designs can be protected in the EU automatically for 3 years after first being shown, and up to 25 years if registered.
- Israeli law: copyright protects expression only. "Passing off" (making people believe you are the other brand) is the real danger. Since a 2008 Supreme Court ruling, copying an unprotected idea alone is usually not enough for a claim.
- Apple App Store review rejects "copycat" apps. This is the risk most likely to hit first, before any lawsuit.

**What it means for us**
- Keep the flows and features. Make colours, type, card shape, icon style, illustration style and every sentence our own. The current design system (espresso, Aileron and Heebo, our tokens in `docs/design.md`) is a good start.
- The "Vinted UX deconstruction" that `CLAUDE.md` says to follow where nothing is decided is fine as a reference for how things work. Do not lift its screens or wording.
- This was general research, not legal advice.

## Home feed plan (planning session, branch `main`, no code)

### What was done

- Planned a home feed that learns from what people do: hearts, opens, coming back to the same item, category visits, searches. Three helpers worked side by side: one mapped our code, one read how other marketplaces do it, one designed the system. The plan is in [docs/plans/home-feed.md](../plans/home-feed.md).
- The main finding: the app already has a simple personal feed (it pushes up items from followed sellers, hearted categories and brands, and your sizes). It does not learn from opens or visits, old hearts never fade, and a page can fill with one seller.
- The plan in short: each person gets a taste profile (scores for categories, brands, sizes, price bands) that grows with each action and fades by half after two weeks. The feed mixes taste, new items, popular items and a few surprises, with variety rules and a short "why you see this" reason on each card. People can hide items, reset their taste or switch it off.
- Added a "behind the scenes" section: everything stays in our Postgres database, only five new indexes (lookups that make one kind of question fast), nightly cleanup, four health numbers to watch.
- A first version of the plan was also published as an interactive page (private artifact). Eden asked for a plain document instead, so the Markdown file is the one to use.

- **Vercel checks the code more loosely than the Mac.** Three small spots only passed the strict check. Repeating "strict" in the server's own settings did not help (Vercel ignores it), so the three spots now pass both ways. `5a22091`

The server and those folders are now packed into one file before upload. The first try put the file in a "dist" folder, which made Vercel serve it as a plain website file. It now sits at the top as `server.mjs`, which Vercel picks first. `1194fe2`, `2da0072`
- **Every request hung for 60 seconds.** While loading the server, Vercel swaps "start listening" for a stand-in that never says done, and our startup waited for it. It no longer waits. `c1fa141`
- **The first table step went to the Mac's database, not the online one.** The database tool loads the local settings first and the online file could not replace them. The online file is now read on its own. No harm done, the Mac already had every table. `ed31472`
- **The code email never appeared.** The email is sent after the answer goes back, and Vercel froze the server at that moment. Vercel's "keep running until this finishes" now covers code emails and two photo clean ups. `2457d36`
- Lesson: on Vercel, anything that runs after the answer must be wrapped, and anything the server waits for while loading must not depend on the platform.
